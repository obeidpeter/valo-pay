# The console: design rationale

This records why the console is the way it is: the landing and sign-in pages first, then each state and each audit in the sections below, so the next change is made against the same reasoning rather than against taste. It follows the design-rationale practice from the interaction-design course notes: explain the decisions, keep the design knowledge, and make the discipline visible. The principles applied are Nielsen's ten usability heuristics, the visual foundations from the first interaction-design lecture (information hierarchy, Gestalt proximity, small multiples, white space, grids, asymmetric typography, greyscale first, one or two colours, animation sparingly, keep it simple), and the second lecture's Norman principles, Dix et al. usability principles, Shneiderman's eight golden rules and universal design.

Files: the landing and sign-in pages (`artifacts/valo-pay/src/pages/landing.tsx`, `src/pages/sign-in.tsx`, `src/components/brand.tsx`), the landing sections and scoped styles (`src/components/landing-sections.tsx`, `src/landing.css`, `src/landing-sections.css`), the shell and routing (`src/components/layout.tsx`, `src/App.tsx`), the shared notices and states (`src/components/notice.tsx`, `error-boundary.tsx`, `workspace-unavailable.tsx`, `loading.tsx`, `empty-state.tsx`, `form-field.tsx`, `src/lib/notify.tsx`), the theme and the formatters (`src/lib/theme.ts`, `src/lib/formatters.ts`) and the stylesheet (`src/index.css`); each section below names what it changed. Tests: `artifacts/valo-pay/tests`, one file per section.

## Who the pages are for

The visitor is the buyer the marketing strategy describes: a head of collections, a finance lead or a founder at a Nigerian lender that collects by direct debit. They arrive with five questions, in this order: What is this? Is it for me? What does it do, and what does it not do? What does it cost and is it safe? How do I get in? The landing page answers them in that order, top to bottom, so the information appears in the natural and logical order the visitor expects (Nielsen 2, information hierarchy).

The second reader is someone who already has a workspace and wants to sign in, and the third is a reviewer, regulator or partner who wants the boundary statements. The header serves the second reader in one click, and the "What we are, and what we are not" section serves the third without making the first reader scroll past it.

## The conceptual model on the page

Valo Pay is an operations layer that observes and records what the lender's aggregator and loan software do; it is not a wallet, a bank or a payment provider. The page carries that model in three places: the descriptor beside the name ("Collections operations layer"), the three steps of "How it works" (plug in, observe, instruct within the rules) with a beginning, middle and end, and the boundaries section that says what we are not before anyone has to ask.

The sandbox and the workspace are the same console. The sandbox is anonymous and synthetic; a workspace is the same console that remembers you. The page never blurs the two: "Open the sandbox" and "Sign in" are different words, different buttons and different addresses, and the helper line under the hero says the sandbox needs no sign-in.

## Information hierarchy and the visual foundations

- **The first three lines.** Line one is the descriptor ("A collections operations layer for lenders that collect by direct debit"), line two the promise (the H1), line three begins "We never hold money." The name says "Pay", so the marketing strategy requires the descriptor and that sentence in the first three lines, and the test pins them there.
- **One hierarchy of type.** One H1, one H2 per section and H3s for the jobs, process steps and audiences. Weight and size carry the hierarchy, so the page still reads in greyscale (design first in greyscale, then add colour).
- **Left-aligned, asymmetric layout.** The landing uses a 1240 px maximum container. The desktop hero places left-aligned copy beside a static reconciliation preview with two matched payments and one proposed match awaiting review. It is an illustration using sample records, not an interactive dashboard. The summary and payment rows keep a consistent reading edge as the layout stacks.
- **Grid and spacing.** The jobs use four columns on desktop, two on tablet and one on a phone. The three process columns become one on a phone. The trust heading sits beside four stacked boundary cards; the four audience cards follow the same four-to-two-to-one progression as the jobs. Section spacing narrows with the viewport, and card padding keeps each icon, heading and explanation together.
- **Proximity.** What belongs together sits together: the icon, title and text of a card; the two buttons and their helper line; the artwork and its caption. White space separates sections, while fine process dividers and faint decorative contours support the reference's visual rhythm.
- **Small multiples.** The jobs share one card treatment, the process steps one column treatment, and the boundary statements one compact row treatment. Each group uses consistent shapes so content differences remain easy to compare.
- **White space as an element.** Airy desktop sections become more compact on small screens. The hero preview uses rows in normal document flow. Faint contours and the final call to action's orange arcs are CSS decorations, not raster assets; they stay behind the content and do not move.
- **Colour.** The landing has a scoped `--lp-*` palette: warm paper, white cards, navy ink, muted text and an accessible darker orange (`--lp-orange: #c63f0b` in light mode). Orange text and actions use that darker treatment; the brighter brand orange remains decorative. The landing defines dark equivalents without changing the console or sign-in palette. The console's burnt orange (`--primary`, 6.0:1 on white) remains its action colour. Green is a status cue paired with words, including the preview's "Matched" statuses; a separate "Needs review" item remains visible.
- **Animation.** None. Buttons change colour on hover and focus; nothing moves, slides or fades in. Animation draws the eye, and on this page the eye should be on the words.
- **Keep it simple.** Every link says where it goes: "Open the sandbox", "Sign in", "See how it works", "Back to the start". No "Learn more", no "Get started", no icon-only buttons.

## Norman's principles

- **Visibility and discoverability.** The ways in are visible in the header, the hero and the end of the page. No hamburger menu holds the only way in; on a phone the header keeps Sign in, and the hero's Open the sandbox is in the first screen.
- **Affordance and signifiers.** Buttons use the established Button component and links look like links. The static hero preview presents readable payment rows and status labels; it contains no controls that could be mistaken for a working console.
- **Mapping.** The three steps are numbered in the order they happen. "See how it works" scrolls to "How it works".
- **Feedback.** Hover and focus states on every control; the page title changes per page; when the sandbox opens the banner and the lender selector confirm where you are.
- **Constraints.** There are two actions, and neither can move money or change a record. The text says so.
- **Knowledge in the world.** The four jobs, the price and the boundaries are on the page rather than behind a form or in a deck.
- **Simplify the task structure.** Opening the sandbox is one click and needs no account; signing in is one form with no wizard around it.

## Dix et al.: learnability, flexibility, robustness

- **Learnability.** Predictability: each link's label is its destination. Synthesisability: the title and address change on every page, and the console's banner confirms the state. Familiarity: the words are the lender's own. Generalisability: the sign-in shell reuses the console's card, button and status chip, so what is learnt on one page applies to the next. Consistency: one lockup, one action colour, one voice.
- **Flexibility.** Substitutivity: two equivalent ways in, and the sandbox is reachable from the sign-in page as well as the landing page. Customisation lives inside the console's settings, not on these pages.
- **Robustness.** Observability: the status chips, and the figure labelled as an illustration. Recoverability: "Back to the start" on every page and a not-found page inside the console. Responsiveness: the pages are static, fetch nothing and load no late images, so they render before the API answers and never shift layout; the console's own load was measured and cut (Performance, below). Task conformance: the two tasks a visitor has, to read and to get in, are both complete on the page.

## Shneiderman's eight golden rules

Consistency (the same terms on the landing page, the sign-in pages and the console: sandbox, workspace, lender); shortcuts (skip link, anchors, header actions); informative feedback (focus rings, hover states, the console's banner on arrival); closure (how it works has a beginning, a middle and an end, and the page ends with the two ways in); simple error handling (the unavailable-sign-in card, the not-found page); easy reversal (leaving the sign-in page costs nothing, and a sandbox is disposable: removed after 30 days without a change); internal locus of control (nothing plays, scrolls or creates itself; the visitor opens the sandbox); reduced short-term memory load (the sign-in page repeats what signing in changes, the ways in repeat, and there is no multi-step flow).

## Universal design

- **Equitable.** The sandbox needs no account and costs nothing to look at.
- **Perceptible.** In the console, text is ink on the warm page background (about 14:1) or the muted grey (about 5.1:1); the brand orange is never text. The dark theme meets the same bar: 17:1 and 7.8:1 on its background, with the figures for the status colours under Dark mode below. On paper the light theme's ink is used whatever the screen showed, and status is carried by words and borders because backgrounds are not printed by default. Status is carried by words as well as colour, and a status word passes 4.5:1 on its own tint. Icons are hidden from assistive technology and always paired with text. Every control has a name that outlives its placeholder, and a frame that scrolls has a name while it scrolls. Landmarks: header, a labelled section nav, main, aside, footer; headings in order; a `figure` with its caption; the steps are an ordered list whose headings read "Step 1" to a screen reader.
- **Simple and intuitive.** Plain words, short sentences, one idea per line.
- **Tolerance for error.** Nothing on these pages is destructive or irreversible.
- **Low physical effort.** Everything works by keyboard in reading order; buttons are at least 36 px high, and close buttons and link-styled buttons at least 24 px; no drag, hover-only or timed interaction; nothing moves for someone who has asked for less motion.
- **Size and space.** The landing hero stacks at tablet width; its jobs and audience cards retain two columns until phone width, where the process also becomes one column. The preview uses container queries to reduce padding and remove decorative avatars when its column narrows. Its card, matching workflow and caption remain in normal document flow. The illustration's numbers use tabular figures. In the console, below 768 px the sidebar folds into a drawer whose rows are 44 px tall for a thumb, the lender selector stays in the bar, page padding drops to 16 px, every table scrolls within its own frame rather than the page, two-column groups become one column and rows of buttons wrap.

## The sign-in page

Clerk's form is themed rather than rebuilt. It already handles every strategy the account is configured with, its error messages are plain language, and its labels stay visible. Rebuilding it would add a second place where account errors are worded and risk a form that looks like sign-in but is not one. The theming (`appearance` in `sign-in.tsx`) passes the console's colour tokens (the action orange, the ink, the card and the muted grey, so both themes are covered), radius and typeface, so the form belongs to the page.

The page pairs a navy welcome panel with a focused authentication card, using the landing page's warm surfaces and orange accents in both themes. The heading says what the form is for ("Sign in to your workspace"); two short benefits explain saved work and customer history. Workspace retention details are available in a keyboard-accessible disclosure. "We never hold money" remains visible because a shared sign-in link can be the first page a visitor sees. A distinct "Just exploring?" sandbox link sits below the form. On phones, the welcome is compact and the form comes before the supporting benefits in both visual and document order. Sign-up shares the layout with its own introductory copy.

The authentication styles are scoped to the sign-in/sign-up frame. Clerk retains its Google/email flows, account links, validation and recovery screens; its form inputs and primary/social buttons are at least 46 px high, with visible focus styles. Input text is 16 px to avoid automatic form zoom on phones. No credential handling or authentication configuration is changed.

Where this host has no Clerk key there is no account to sign into. The old console redirected `/sign-in` to `/` in that case, which reads as a broken link. The page now says sign-in is not available on this host, says why, and offers the sandbox, instead of a form that could not work (Nielsen 1, 5 and 9). After sign-in Clerk sends the visitor to `/overview`.

## Routing

The landing and sign-in pages are routed outside the console's workspace provider (`App.tsx`). Visiting `/`, `/sign-in` or `/sign-up` therefore sends no request to the API and creates no sandbox; the workspace request happens only when someone opens the console. The overview moved from `/` to `/overview` to make room, and the console's brand lockup links back to `/`. `App.tsx` keeps one list of the console's addresses and mounts the console only for those, so an address it has no page for is answered outside the provider too: a mistyped address or a stray crawler creates no sandbox.

## Not found

There are two not-found states, and they are different pages because the visitor's situation is different. An address the console has no page for gets the public frame (the same header as the sign-in pages), the address it looked for shown exactly so a typo can be seen, a sentence that nothing changed, and two ways out: the overview and the start. It fetches nothing, so it renders at once and creates nothing. A customer address whose record the current lender does not have is a page that exists with a record that does not, so it stays inside the console with the sidebar and the lender selector, names the reference it looked for, says the customer may belong to another lender in the workspace, and links back to the customer list. Neither state shows a code or a stack trace, and each sets a page title that says what happened (Nielsen 1 and 9, Dix recoverability, Shneiderman's simple error handling).

## Errors

When a page throws, the console does not go blank and does not show a developer's message. The boundary inside the console layout catches it, so the sidebar and the lender selector stay as the way out, and the page area shows the same notice card as the not-found states with `role="alert"` so it is announced; the page title says "Page error" while the notice is up. It says what happened (the page stopped working), what it means (an error on a page changes no record, and the audit log holds the result of any action just confirmed), what to report if it happens again (the time and the address, shown exactly), and two ways on: Try again, which re-renders the page, and the overview, as a plain link so it starts afresh. The old fallback said "This part of the app hit an error" in grey outside the design system and printed nothing a lender's staff could act on. The error's own message can carry an API response or another internal, so it is folded away under "Technical details" in development and not rendered in production. Boundaries outside the console (the router and the root) show the same notice in the public frame, and navigating to another address clears a caught error, so a broken page never traps the visitor (Nielsen 3 and 9, Dix recoverability, Shneiderman's simple error handling and easy reversal). The not-found states and the error notice share one card, `components/notice.tsx`, so every such moment reads the same way.

When the workspace itself cannot be loaded, nothing in the console can work, so the notice replaces the pages rather than sitting inside them, in the same frame and card. The words follow the situation instead of one catch-all sentence: when the service asks the visitor to wait (a new sandbox refused by the per-address limit, or the request limit), its own plain-language message is passed on; when there is no answer, the page says the console could not reach the service and to check the connection; when the service hit an error, it says so and gives the time for a report, and keeps the body's wording to itself because a 5xx can carry internals. Every version says that no lender data has been changed. Try again shows its own progress ("Trying again…", disabled) so a click is seen to do something, and the frame keeps the start and, where sign-in exists, sign-in as the other ways on. The old state was one centred sentence with a retry button and no way out. Before the workspace arrives, the page area says "Loading your workspace…" as a status rather than showing pages with nothing in them (Nielsen 1: visibility of system status; 9; Dix: observability and recoverability; Shneiderman: informative feedback).

## Loading and waiting

Waiting is a state like any other, so it is said, not left blank. Every region that waits for the API shows one status line, `Loading <what>…`, that names what is coming ("Loading the overview…", "Loading due items…"), left-aligned in the space the content will take, with `role="status"` so it is announced once. The only motion on the page is the small spinner beside it, and it stops under reduced motion. A table that is waiting says so in its own row, so the header and the columns are already in place. Before this the console had five wordings across twenty-two places ("Loading...", "Generating reports..." for a fetch, "Loading audit logs..."), three paddings, centred text, an optional pulse and no status role.

No skeletons. A line of words is simpler, the same everywhere, honest about how long it may take, and it never pretends to know the shape of what is coming; a pulse of grey boxes is animation that draws the eye for no information (animation sparingly; keep it simple).

A button whose action is in flight is disabled, `aria-busy`, and its label says what is happening beside a spinner: Saving…, Generating…, Verifying…, Running the engine…, Closing the day…, Trying again…. This is one property of the button (`busy` with `busyLabel`) rather than a ternary rewritten in each page, so the feedback is the same everywhere and the click cannot be repeated while it runs. Where several buttons share one action, such as the dispute pack's PDF, CSV and JSON, only the one that was pressed says it is generating and the others wait, disabled (Nielsen 1: visibility of system status; Dix: responsiveness and observability; Shneiderman: informative feedback; universal design: perceptible information, reduced motion).


## Empty states

An empty list is a message, so it says three things in order: what would be here, why it is empty here and now, and what to do next when there is something to do. "No proposals waiting for review" is followed by what a proposal is and that running the engine looks for new ones; "No mandates yet" says where mandates come from and offers the sandbox's own way to create one; "No daily close yet" points at the button above and the scheduled close. The words are the lender's (instalment, mandate, settlement batch, notice) and every state that has a rule behind it names the rule (REC-09, BIL-01, MEA-05), so the empty state also teaches how the console fills. Before this there were about twenty-six of them with mixed wording ("Log empty", "No authorizations found for this workspace", "No close snapshots available."), centred, some with a large faded icon, and none said what to do.

"Nothing yet" and "nothing matches" are different situations. A search or a filter with no results is the outcome of what the visitor just did, so it says so with the term it looked for ("No customers match “zzzz”", "Nothing resolved yet"), suggests how to widen it, and carries `role="status"` so it is announced; a list that is genuinely empty is not announced, because nothing happened. The customers and audit pages used to show "No customers found" and "Log empty" for both.

One component, `components/empty-state.tsx`, with a table-row form, left-aligned in the space the content will take, without an illustration: the words are the information, and a faded icon adds nothing a reader can act on (Nielsen 1, 2, 6 and 9; Norman: knowledge in the world; Dix: observability; Shneiderman: closure; keep it simple).


## Forms and validation

A form that refuses a value says which one, where it is, and what would be accepted. Every form in the console (the record dialog that most pages use, the mandate form, the execution settings) now checks its fields before asking the server: a missing value is named at the field in the field's own words ("Enter the Full Name.", "Choose the Customer.", "Enter the close time as HH:MM in West Africa Time, for example 07:00."), the control is marked invalid at its edge and in the accessibility tree, the message is linked to it so a screen reader reads both together, focus moves to the first field that needs attention, and one alert at the top says how many fields need attention so the situation is announced once. A field being corrected drops its message at once. Nothing is sent until the form is complete, so a slip never costs a request or a round trip (Nielsen 5: error prevention; 9: plain words, the problem stated precisely, a constructive way out; Dix: recoverability; Shneiderman: simple error handling; universal design: perceptible, never colour alone).

What only the server can refuse is treated the same way. Its validation details name fields by their path in the request, and each one is placed under the field it names; a rule or a role refusal that names no field becomes the alert's title in the server's own plain words. The execution settings do the same with the server's rule messages, which begin with the key they concern, so "closeTime must be…" sits under the close time rather than in a toast that disappears. Before this, the record dialog listed raw field paths below the form, the mandate form printed the HTTP status line, and the settings page announced a rejection in a toast and then hid it.

Native browser validation is switched off on these forms because its bubbles differ by browser, cannot be styled to match, and are not announced consistently; the `required` attribute stays for its meaning. Required fields keep the conventional asterisk, and a button that waits for a value says why (the kill switch's reason is required and recorded in the audit log).


## Notifications

A notice after an action is either a confirmation or a problem, and the two behave differently on purpose. A done notice says what happened in plain words and where the result is ("Dispute pack generated. It opened in a new tab. SHA-256 …, generated 18 Sept 2026, 09:08"), is announced politely so it does not interrupt, and goes away on its own after six seconds, pausing while pointed at or focused. A problem notice says what did not happen and passes on the server's own words ("The dispute pack was not generated. Packs are limited to five a day for this customer. Nothing has been changed."), is announced at once, and stays until dismissed, because an error that disappears before it is read is no error message at all. Before this every notice, errors included, vanished after five seconds, the dismiss control was invisible until hovered, and one notice replaced another. The dismiss control is now always visible and named, three notices can stand at once, and nothing animates under reduced motion.

Two decisions follow from the same rule that a notice is for what the page cannot show. The audit page shows the result of a verification in its own status box, so it no longer raises a notice for it, only for a request that failed. The dispute pack opens in a new tab, which the page cannot show, so its notice says so and carries an Open action for a browser that kept the tab closed. And no notice ever carries an HTTP status line: `saidBy` passes on the server's sentence or a plain fallback (Nielsen 1, 3, 8 and 9; Shneiderman: informative feedback, easy reversal; universal design: perceptible information, low physical effort).


## Keyboard

Everything in the console works without a mouse, and the keyboard paths are the same on every page. The first tab stop on every page, public or console, is a skip link to the page's content, so a keyboard user is not made to pass the banner, the lender selector and eleven links each time. After in-app navigation focus moves to the page's main region, as it would on a page load, so keyboard and screen-reader users start at the top of what changed rather than on a link that may no longer exist; the current page's link carries `aria-current`, so "current page" is announced and not only coloured. Every sidebar link shows a focus ring.

Two accelerators exist for the people who use the console all day, and neither is required: "/" puts the caret in the search box on the pages that have one, with a small "/" hint at the edge of the box so it can be found rather than guessed, and Escape clears the box. The exception filter tabs follow the WAI-ARIA tabs pattern: one tab stop for the group, the arrow keys and Home and End move the selection and the focus together, and the list follows. Dialogs, menus and notices already trap and return focus and close on Escape through their primitives, and F8 jumps to the notices. The shortcuts are listed in a Keyboard section on the settings page, because help the reader has to guess at is no help (Nielsen 4, 6, 7 and 10; Shneiderman: shortcuts for frequent users, consistency; universal design: operable by keyboard, low physical effort).

## Responsive layout

The console is used at a desk, but a collections lead checks an exception from a phone and a reviewer opens a shared link on a tablet, and the same page has to work in each place without a second design to learn. One layout adapts: below 768 px the sidebar folds into a drawer behind a Menu button, and above it the sidebar returns. Nothing is added or taken away in between; things move.

**The phone bar.** It holds the three things a page cannot do without: the brand mark (the way back to the start, the same lockup as the sidebar, with the name shown once there is room for it), the lender selector, and the Menu button. The lender stays in the bar rather than in the drawer because every figure on every page belongs to one lender, and a value that changes the meaning of the page must stay visible (Norman: visibility; Nielsen 1). The button says "Menu" in words beside its icon, because an icon alone is a guess (Keep it simple: no icon-only buttons). The bar stays at the top of the screen, so on a long page the menu and the lender are never a scroll away.

**A drawer, not a select.** The console used to offer its pages on a phone as a `<select>`. A select is compact, but it hides the list until it is opened, shows no icons, cannot mark the current page the way the sidebar does, and on the customer timeline had no page to show at all. The drawer lists the same eleven pages, in the same order, with the same icons and the same current-page mark as the sidebar, so what is learnt at a desk holds on a phone (Nielsen 4 and 6; Dix: generalisability; Shneiderman: consistency). Its rows are 44 px tall because they are pressed with a thumb (universal design: size and space). It is a dialog: it takes focus when it opens; Escape, its close button or a tap outside closes it and returns focus to the Menu button; choosing a page closes it and puts focus on the page content, which is exactly what the sidebar does, so there is one keyboard path (Keyboard, above). If the window grows past the breakpoint while it is open it closes, since the button that would close it is no longer shown.

**Tables scroll within their frame, never the page.** A table wider than a phone is allowed to be wide; a collections table with nine columns should not be rebuilt as cards that hide six of them. Each table sits in its own horizontally scrolling frame, so the heading, the filters and the rest of the page stay put and only the table moves; the browser makes the frame reachable from the keyboard when it overflows. Two lists on the reconciliation page scrolled vertically only and now scroll both ways.

**Rows wrap, grids collapse, dialogs fit.** Rows of buttons (the reports header, an experiment's actions) wrap onto a second line rather than run off the page; the settings' two-column groups are one column below 640 px; the persona and emergency controls stack into a column with each control full width, so the kill switch's button is on the screen and not to the right of the reason box where a phone could not show it; the exception filter tabs wrap rather than being clipped by their card. A tablet is the narrow case in disguise: from 768 px the sidebar takes 256 px of the width, so the settings' pairs of fields split again only from 1280 px and the emergency controls stay stacked until 1024 px, because a reason box three characters wide is no box at all. Dialogs fit within 90% of the viewport height and scroll inside.

**Less banner, the same warning.** On a phone the sandbox banner keeps "Sandbox · Synthetic data. We never hold money." on one line and drops the restatement and the MODE badge, which said the same word again; from 640 px the full sentence and the badge return. Page padding is 16 px on a phone, 24 px on a tablet and 32 px at a desk, from the same spacing scale as everything else.

What we did not do: no separate phone pages, no hidden columns, no menu icon without a word beside it, no gesture without a button that does the same. At 200% zoom on a laptop the console becomes the phone layout, which is the point of having one layout (universal design: flexibility in use).

## Dark mode

The console follows the device. Someone whose phone or laptop is set to dark gets a dark console without being asked, because the device already holds the answer and a question would be one more thing between them and the page (Nielsen 7; universal design: flexibility in use). Someone who wants the console to differ from the device chooses Light or Dark on the settings page, and the choice is kept in that browser: it belongs to the person and the screen, not to the workspace, so it is not a setting the API knows about, it works without an account and before any request, and two people sharing a workspace do not fight over it. "Follow the device" gives the choice back (Nielsen 3). The settings page says which theme is showing and why, "Following the device: dark now", so the state is never a guess (Nielsen 1).

**One set of tokens, two values each.** Every colour in the console is a token (`--background`, `--card`, `--muted-foreground` and so on), and the dark theme is the same tokens with dark values, applied by one class on the root element. There is no second stylesheet and no per-page override, so a component looks right in both themes because it was written against the tokens once (Nielsen 4). Status colours were the exception: the sandbox banner, the severity and confidence pills, the alert tints and the warning icons used fixed ambers, blues, reds and greens, which would have stayed as light strips and pale chips on a dark page. They are tokens now: for warning and for info a surface, its text, its border and a strong colour for icons and words on the page, each with a light value that is the colour it always had and a dark value chosen for contrast. Success became a colour that passes as text: the old green was 2.2:1 on white, and "proven", "SIGNED" and the allocated amount were written in it; it is now 7.2:1 in light and 11:1 in dark.

**Contrast in both themes.** On the dark background the main text is 17:1 and the muted grey 7.8:1; the strong warning, info and success colours are 10.8:1, 7.3:1 and 10.5:1; the text on the warning and info surfaces is 13.5:1 and 12.1:1 in dark and 8.3:1 and 8.5:1 in light; the destructive red is 6.0:1 in light and, in dark, 5.6:1 on the card and 4.9:1 on its own tint over it, where the destructive button carries dark text for the same reason the success button does. The muted grey is 5.1:1 on the light page background and 5.4:1 on a card. The brand orange moves from 50% to 55% lightness in the dark theme so the mark keeps its weight on a dark card, and it is still never used for text (Colour, above).

**No flash, and the door matches the room.** A few lines in the page shell apply the choice before the first paint, with the same rule and key as the module the app uses, so a dark device never sees a white page first; a test pins the two together. Native controls follow through `color-scheme`, so a select or a scrollbar is not a light box on a dark page. The sign-in form, which is Clerk's, receives its dark base theme and the console's dark tokens when the console is dark.

What we did not do: no schedule (dark after sunset), because the device does that better; no third high-contrast theme, since both themes meet 4.5:1 for text; no per-page or per-lender colour, because the lender is chosen by name, not by hue.

## Print

A page gets printed when it leaves the console: a customer's timeline for a file, the day's exceptions for a meeting, an audit extract for a question from an auditor. Paper cannot be scrolled, clicked or switched, so the print rules keep what a reader needs later and drop what an operator needs now (Nielsen 2: the printout reads like a file note, not a screenshot; Nielsen 8: nothing on the page that cannot be used on paper).

**What goes.** The sidebar, the phone bar, the banner, every button, field, tab and menu, and every notice and dialog. Links keep their words and lose their underline, because a name in a table is information and a link on paper is not. The settings page's appearance and keyboard help stay on the screen, where they are of use.

**What opens.** Every table scrolls within its frame on screen; on paper a frame that scrolls is a table cut in half, so the frames open to full width, long identifiers wrap, and the header row repeats at the top of each page. Height limits on rendered lists are removed. Paginated tables and timelines print only the current page, with a range and total and an explicit notice when other pages are omitted; printing does not fetch or render the other pages. Rows, list items and figures are not split across pages, and a heading is not left at the foot of a page without what it names. The overview's metrics and its two queues keep their columns, since the paper is wide enough for them and the reader expects the screen's arrangement.

**What paper says that the screen said elsewhere.** The banner and the sidebar carried the sandbox notice and the lender; on paper a line at the top carries both ("Valo Pay · synthetic sandbox", the lender, "Synthetic data. We never hold money. Nothing on this page is live evidence or a statement of account"), and a line at the foot says when the page was printed, from which page and for which lender. The time is taken again as the print dialog opens, because a page can sit open for a day before someone prints it (Nielsen 1: a printout that says what it is and when). Where a list was filtered or searched, the filter is printed as words ("Showing: High severity (1)", "Search: “Ada”"), so the reader knows the page is a part and which part.

**Ink.** A printout is always the light theme: the dark values and the dark variant are scoped to screens, so a dark console prints dark ink on white rather than pale ink on a background the printer would leave out. Backgrounds are not forced, and the page does not need them: every status is carried by a word and every group by a border (greyscale first). Type is set at 11 pt on the console's own scale, with A4 margins of 16 by 14 mm.

**What we left out.** No address printed after each link (a sandbox address on paper helps no one); no "Print this page" button (the browser has one, and a second is one more control to explain); no separate print layout per page (one set of rules over the same markup, so a page that works on screen works on paper without a second design to keep in step).

## Accessibility audit

The pages were built against the principles above; the audit checks them against the standard, WCAG 2.2 at level AA, with axe-core over every public page, every console page and the states a page can be in (the record dialog, the settings form, a filtered list, the phone drawer), in both themes and at desk and phone widths, followed by checks axe does not make: heading levels, landmark names, a keyboard walk that records whether each stop shows its focus, the size of every target, and what still moves when the device asks for less motion. The findings, and what changed:

- **Content outside a landmark.** The sandbox banner and the phone bar sat above the landmarks. They are now the page's header, so a screen-reader user who moves by landmark meets the sandbox notice first, as a sighted user does (WCAG 1.3.1; Nielsen 1).
- **Controls without names.** The execution settings' labels were beside their fields but not attached to them; the persona and import selects and the kill-switch reason had no name at all; the two search boxes had a placeholder for a name, which goes as soon as something is typed. Every label now points at its control, every select and field has a name, the reason has a visible label, and the search boxes are named whatever they hold (WCAG 1.3.1, 3.3.2, 4.1.2).
- **Text that did not pass on its own tint.** The success and destructive pills, a status word on a 10% tint of its own colour, were at 4.4:1 and 4.1:1, and the evidence page's list of missing requirements, drawn at 80% of the destructive colour, was at 3.7:1. The light success colour is now green-800 (7.2:1 on white, 6.1:1 on the tint), the light destructive colour is two shades darker (6.0:1 on white, 5.1:1 on the tint), and the list uses the full colour (WCAG 1.4.3). The selected exception tab lost its dark ground under the pointer and showed white on grey at 1.2:1; it keeps its ground now.
- **Regions that scroll but could not be reached.** A table wider than a phone and the timeline's event list scrolled within a frame that took no focus, so a keyboard could not scroll them. Every frame now becomes a named, focusable region while it scrolls, and nothing while it does not, so a table that fits adds no tab stop (WCAG 2.1.1; `components/scroll-frame.tsx`).
- **A skipped heading level** on the customer timeline, an h3 straight after the h1, is an h2 (WCAG 1.3.1, 2.4.6).
- **Targets under 24 px.** The close button of every dialog and of the drawer was 16 px square; it is 24. The link-styled buttons in lists (Edit, Submit, Approve, Add evidence, the sample download, the gate items) were 16 to 20 px high; they are at least 24 (WCAG 2.5.8).
- **Motion that ignored the device.** The drawer, the dialogs and the overview's fade-in still animated under "reduce motion". One rule now completes every animation and transition at once for anyone who asked for that, keeping the end-of-animation events the dialogs and notices wait for (WCAG 2.3.3).

What the audit found sound: one main, one header, a named navigation and a complementary sidebar on every console page; one h1 per page; every keyboard stop showing a focus ring, across 14 to 24 stops per page; every button and link with a name; the tabs, dialogs and notices carrying their roles and states. What remains flagged and stays: the focus guards the dialog library places around an open dialog, which axe cannot judge and which are its own mechanism for keeping focus inside; and the 16 px native radio buttons on the settings page, whose labels are their targets.

The audit runs again in the tests (`tests/a11y.test.tsx`: axe over every page and the dialog and form states, without the rules that need a real layout) and by hand in a browser before a change to the pages ships (How to check a change).

## Performance

Responsiveness is one of Dix's three parts of robustness, and a lender's staff reach the console on the connections they have. The audit measured before changing anything: the bundle's composition from its source map, and in Chromium, cold loads of the landing page and the console (requests, bytes, first paint, largest paint, layout shift, long tasks), the same on a slow connection with a slow processor (1.6 Mb/s down, 150 ms latency, the processor at a quarter), a walk through the pages counting the requests each made, and a timeline of the first visit to a page. Then the changes the numbers justified, and the measurements again.

**What was found.** One script of 712 kB (201 kB compressed) carried every page, and the landing page, which is for a visitor on an unknown connection, carried the whole console: on the slow connection it painted at 4.8 s and the overview at 5.6 s. The typefaces were asked for from inside the stylesheet, so the browser learned of them only after the stylesheet had arrived, and the page shell also asked for a typeface nothing uses. Every return to a page fetched its data again, so a walk between three pages made a request for the overview three times in twenty seconds. A tooltip provider sat at the root of the app for tooltips that no page shows. The scrolling frames from the accessibility audit created a new observer on every render.

**What changed.** The landing page and the shell ship in one chunk of 466 kB (141 kB compressed); every console page and the sign-in pages are their own chunks, fetched on first visit; the remaining pages are fetched while the browser is idle, the overview from the landing page and every page from the console, unless the browser asks to save data or reports a 2G connection. The typefaces are linked from the page shell, in parallel with the stylesheet, and the unused one is gone. Data fetched in the last thirty seconds is shown at once when a page is returned to, instead of a loading line and a repeated request; an action's invalidation still refetches what it changed, and a tab that comes back after longer refetches on focus. The tooltip provider is gone. Each scrolling frame observes once.

**A hold that was not the network.** With the pages split, the first visit to a page took 330 ms on a fast connection although its code arrived in 20 ms. The timeline showed why: React commits a Suspense fallback and then holds it for 300 ms before the content may replace it, to avoid a flicker. The pages therefore load through plain state instead of Suspense: the page area says "Loading the page…" until the code is here and renders the page the moment it is, and a chunk that cannot be fetched is thrown to the error boundary. The first visit to a page now renders 40 ms after the click.

**Before and after.** Landing page: 712 kB of script became 466 kB; first paint on the slow connection 4.8 s became 3.5 s; on a fast one 384 ms became 380 ms. Overview: largest paint on the slow connection 5.6 s became 4.7 s. A page returned to within thirty seconds: one request became none, and the page appears at once. A page visited for the first time: 330 ms became 40 ms after the click. The overview's own largest paint on a fast connection moved from 968 ms to about 1,000 ms, the one round trip its chunk now costs on a direct visit; the prefetch removes it for every page visited after the first. No layout shift above 0.02 on any page, and no long task on a fast connection.

**What we left out.** Clerk's 87 kB stays in the shell, because the deployment signs people in and the anonymous sandbox is the exception; splitting it would need the sign-in state to be loaded before any page renders. The shared API client (69 kB) stays a chunk of its own that the pages share, rather than being folded into the shell, so the landing page does not carry it; the three tiny icon chunks that follow from the split cost a few hundred bytes each and load alongside the page that needs them. Cache and compression headers for the built files belong to the host that serves them, not to this repository. No service worker: the console is used live, and a stale copy of it would be worse than a slow one.


## Internationalisation

Norman's conceptual model and Nielsen's second heuristic both ask that the system speak the user's language, and for a Nigerian lender that language has conventions a browser will not supply on its own: British English words, day-month-year dates on a 24-hour clock, the naira with its sign, West Africa Time, and names written with the marks their bearers use (Adéyẹmí, Ọbi, Ṣeyi). The audit read every place the console and the API turn a value into words, set the browser to other languages and zones (German in New York, American English in Los Angeles) to see what changed, and looked at what the typefaces and the exports can spell.

**What was found.** Dates and amounts already had one locale and one zone (the formatters fix Africa/Lagos, British English for dates and Nigerian English for money), so a viewer in New York saw the same "18 Sept 2026, 13:50" as a viewer in Lagos; but no timestamp said which zone it was in, so the New York viewer had no way to know the time was not theirs, and a value that was only a day (a due date) was shown with an invented time of 01:00. One table formatted a number with the browser's own locale (20.000 in a German browser). Counts were built as "N items", so the audit log reported "1 entries" and a close "1 observations received". A search compared letters exactly, so "obi" could not find "Ọbi", nor "adeyemi" "Adéyẹmí". The synthetic names carried no marks at all, which hid the next two findings: the dispute pack's PDF used the standard Helvetica, which encodes WinAnsi only, and printed "?" for every ẹ, ọ or ṣ in a name, on a document meant as evidence of who was written to; and the CSV exports, UTF-8 without a byte order mark, open in a spreadsheet program as mojibake for the same letters. The page declared its language as generic English.

**What changed.** Every timestamp names its zone ("18 Sept 2026, 13:50 WAT"), and a day is shown as a day. The formatters carry the policy in one place (`lib/formatters.ts`): British English for words and dates, Nigerian conventions for numbers and money, West Africa Time for every instant whatever the device is set to; the one stray `toLocaleString()` now goes through them. A count helper gives a noun its number ("1 entry", "0 items", "1,234 records"), in the console and in the API's close summaries, invoice statements, alerts and packs alike. A search folds case and marks on both sides, so a name is found however it was typed. The synthetic lenders' customers are written as their bearers write them (Túndé, Ọbi, Adéyẹmí, Ifẹ, Ṣeyi), so every screen, export and test meets those letters. The PDF exports embed their own typeface, a Latin subset of Liberation Sans that is metrically the same as Helvetica, so nothing moved on the page, with the dot-below letters, the tone marks and the naira sign; it travels as base64 in a source module, since the repository's snapshot tool carries source text only, and the documents declare their language too. CSV downloads start with the byte order mark, which the importer already skipped. The page declares `lang="en-GB"`, the spelling it uses, so a screen reader picks a British voice and hyphenation and quotation follow British rules; Nigerian English was considered, and no assistive technology carries a voice for it, so the tag would have fallen back to generic English.

**Under other settings.** With the browser set to German in New York, and to American English in Los Angeles, the console showed "19 Sept 2026, 07:00 WAT (daily)" for the next close and "18 Sept 2026, 13:50 WAT" on the audit log, exactly as it did set to Nigerian English in Lagos, while the device's own rendering of one instant was "18.9.2026, 18:30:00" in the first and "9/18/2026, 3:30:00 PM" in the second.

**The typefaces.** Plus Jakarta Sans, the console's face, carries ẹ, ọ, ṣ, Ṣ, the combining tone marks and the naira sign in the subsets Google Fonts serves (checked in the font files themselves); the arrow and "at least" signs in two report chips fall to the system font. Spline Sans Mono, the face for references and amounts, has no naira sign, so the ₦ before an amount is drawn by the device's monospace face beside Spline's digits: legible, one glyph, and left as it is; the cure would be a mono face that has the sign, or the sans with tabular figures for amounts, which is a typographic decision rather than a correctness one. In the PDF the embedded face draws every letter.

**What we left out.** No translation layer: the product has one language, its strings live in the components, and a message catalogue for a second language would be a project of its own; the API's messages are English prose without codes for the same reason. No right-to-left layout: the styles say left and right rather than start and end in about 270 places, a mechanical change (ml to ms, pr to pe, text-right to text-end, and so on) for the day a right-to-left market is in scope, not before. The uppercase labels transform English only, where the transform is safe. The identifiers the API orders with `localeCompare` are lowercase hexadecimal and ISO timestamps, on which every collation agrees.

The narrower requests reduce network payloads; production capacity still requires deployment-specific measurement.

Before a mandate action, the confirmation names the customer and mandate, shows the current and resulting state and explains consequences and recovery. Suspension can be followed by Resume, but cancelled attempts stay cancelled; cancellation requires a new mandate with fresh consent if collection is to resume. Reissue starts with blank consent evidence and offers the new record after completion. These distinctions prevent a generic confirmation from implying that every change can be undone (Nielsen 5 and 9).

Policy and template reviews place the version, available previous values and a synthetic customer-message example beside the decision. Explicit previous-version links take precedence; a uniquely identifiable earlier version may be compared with its basis labelled, and an unavailable or ambiguous predecessor is disclosed. The interface does not invent a comparison or imply that approval sends a message or changes existing mandates (Nielsen 1, 2 and 6).

Temporary customer-history errors offer retry and are distinct from a confirmed missing customer. Audit verification is tied to its lender and check time, clears an old result before a new check, and ignores a response from an earlier visit. A past successful check cannot serve as the status of a new request or another lender (Nielsen 1 and 9).

Daily-close copy distinguishes a requested lender schedule from a running service. A recent process heartbeat permits the next-run display; off, unavailable, starting, failed and delayed states explain what the operator can do. The completed-close history remains separate from the service-check time. Saving the preference does not start the worker, and a healthy heartbeat is not a guarantee that a particular close completed (Nielsen 1).

## Queue continuity and compact close history

Search is an explicit labelled form on the three priority queues. Search and saved-filter changes start on page one; returning from a customer restores the original filters and page. Pages remain shareable within their lender context, and changing lender does not reuse another lender's position. This supports recognition and recovery without browser-stored customer records.

Daily-close summaries use stacked cards on phones and aligned columns on wider screens. Each close has a native disclosure, which supports keyboard navigation and fetches its evidence only when opened. Date filters compare positions across the full range, with clear empty/error states. Print output states that it includes summaries for the current page, while detailed billing disclosures continue to expand for print.
# Clear search outcomes and bounded customer history

A search with no matches now says so instead of implying that the queue is clear or that records must be created. Clear search preserves other filters. Reconciliation search includes the linked references operators already use, and switching its views retains the search (Nielsen 1, 3 and 6).

Customer history requests only the visible pages, with separate controls for mandates, instalments, payments and events. Full counts, unchanged balances and full dispute-pack exports keep the meaning of the customer position independent of pagination. An off-page selected event remains visible in the selected-record card. Page state survives reload and browser navigation (Nielsen 1 and 4).
